* Web3 Architecture
** 0. Staff-level lens
A Web3 product is a hybrid system: a few contracts on a public chain, plus clients, wallets, RPC providers, indexers, backends, storage, oracles, and operational infrastructure. Its architecture is defined by where each responsibility lives and which party must be trusted for it. At Staff and Principal scope you are expected to:
- draw the whole system, including off-chain components and third parties, and mark each trust boundary and each single point of failure;
- decide what goes on-chain (expensive, public, irreversible) and what does not (see =09_on-chain-and-off-chain.org=);
- make decentralization claims precise: who can pause, upgrade, censor, or steal, and how users exit;
- apply ordinary software architecture discipline (boundaries, contracts, evolution, review) to a system where mistakes are irreversible.
Companion files: =09_on-chain-and-off-chain.org=, =13_indexing-and-data-access.org=, =15_deployment-and-upgrades.org=, =18_architecture-decisions.org=. In the Software Architecture notes: styles, boundaries, patterns, quality attributes, and reviews.

** 1. The layers of a typical dApp

- *Contracts*: the minimal set of rules that must be enforced without trust: asset custody, accounting, access control, settlement.
- *Wallets and keys*: where users (and operators) hold keys and sign; the user's root of trust (see =08_wallets-and-cryptography.org=).
- *Frontend*: the interface; often hosted traditionally, sometimes on content-addressed storage with ENS. A compromised frontend can trick users into signing malicious transactions, so frontend integrity is a security control, not a convenience.
- *RPC and node infrastructure*: how clients read state and submit transactions; commercial providers, self-hosted nodes, or both; the largest shared dependency of most dApps.
- *Indexers and data stores*: convert raw logs and state into queryable data (see =13_indexing-and-data-access.org=).
- *Backend services*: APIs, notification, relayers (sponsor gas or submit transactions), keepers (trigger time- or condition-based actions such as liquidations), bots, risk engines, and KYC or compliance services where required.
- *Storage*: content-addressed (IPFS, Arweave, Filecoin) or conventional object storage for metadata and large data.
- *Oracles and cross-chain messaging*: bring external data and messages (see =10_oracles-and-mev.org=, =11_layer-2-and-cross-chain.org=).
- *Operations*: monitoring, alerting, incident response, key management, deployment pipelines (see files 15 and 17).

** 2. Trust boundaries and the decentralization spectrum
- *Make a trust table* for the system: for each function (custody, execution, ordering, upgrades, pausing, price data, data availability, frontend, RPC), who is trusted, what can they do, and what can users do if they misbehave? Include /admin powers/ explicitly: an upgradeable contract controlled by a multisig is a trusted party with a time delay, not a trustless system.
- *Spectrum*: fully centralized service with a token, centralized operator with on-chain settlement, upgradeable protocol with governance and timelocks, immutable core with minimal governance, fully immutable and permissionless. Each step trades flexibility and compliance ability for trust minimization.
- *Progressive decentralization*: launch with training wheels (caps, pausing, upgradeability, allowlists), publish the plan and criteria, and remove powers as the system matures and audits accumulate. State the schedule and keep the promises.
- *Walkaway test*: if the team disappears, can users still exit with their assets and can the system keep running? Provide escape hatches (forced withdrawals, emergency exit modes) where feasible.
- *Credible neutrality and permissionlessness*: who may use or build on the protocol without permission? Gatekeeping (allowlists, blacklists) has regulatory and trust consequences.
- *Honest claims*: do not describe a system as trustless or decentralized if admin keys, a single sequencer, a single RPC, or a single frontend can censor or steal. Users and auditors will test the claim.

** 3. Architectural patterns
- *Minimal on-chain core*: keep contracts small, simple, and stable; push computation, matching, ranking, and storage off-chain with verifiable settlement on-chain.
- *Factory and clones*: deploy many instances cheaply with minimal proxies (EIP-1167) behind a factory; useful for per-user vaults or per-market contracts; each instance shares code, so a bug is replicated everywhere.
- *Modular and plugin architectures*: a core with registries and modules (modules as separately audited units, with permissions); powerful, and a source of complexity and privilege escalation risk. The diamond pattern (EIP-2535) multiplexes many implementations behind one address and increases review burden.
- *Registry and router patterns*: address books, routers that call vetted adapters; keep adapters isolated and bounded.
- *Meta-transactions and sponsored gas*: users sign messages and a relayer submits them (ERC-2771 trusted forwarders), or smart accounts with paymasters (ERC-4337); EIP-7702 lets an EOA delegate to contract code (see =08_wallets-and-cryptography.org=). Each adds a trusted component and new failure modes (relayer downtime, abuse, replay).
- *Commit-reveal and batching*: hide choices until revealed; amortize costs by posting Merkle roots and letting users claim with proofs.
- *Event-driven back ends*: derive application state from chain events with reorg-aware processors (see =13_indexing-and-data-access.org=).
- *Outbox and idempotent submitters* for transaction-sending services (see =12_distributed-systems.org=).
- *Multi-chain deployments*: the same contracts on several chains with consistent addresses (CREATE2 or CREATE3 factories) and carefully chosen messaging between them (see =11_layer-2-and-cross-chain.org=). Each chain has its own security, fee, and finality properties.
- *Account abstraction architectures*: smart accounts for users with social recovery, spending limits, session keys, and batched actions.
- *Custody architectures for operators*: EOA, multisig (for example Safe) with a signer policy, MPC or threshold signing, and HSM or KMS-backed keys; combine with timelocks and role separation.
- *Compliance-aware designs*: allowlists, pausing, freezes for regulated assets (ERC-3643-like permissioned tokens), screening of addresses, and data-protection rules for off-chain personal data. Involve legal counsel early; this note is not legal advice.

** 4. Non-functional requirements specific to Web3
- *Finality and latency*: block time, inclusion latency, finality delay, and L2 confirmation levels determine UX and risk policy (see =06_transactions-and-gas.org=).
- *Availability*: dependence on RPC providers, sequencers, bridges, oracles, indexers, and frontends; design redundancy for each (multi-provider RPC, fallback frontends, alternative data sources).
- *Cost*: gas per user action, L1 data costs for rollups, infrastructure costs, and oracle fees; model cost per action at expected volume and at gas spikes.
- *Security*: assets, keys, admin powers, supply chain; the cost of failure is permanent.
- *Privacy*: public chain data links activity to addresses; off-chain systems and compliance needs interact with this.
- *Composability*: other protocols will integrate yours; your interfaces and token behaviors become dependencies for them.
- *Maintainability and evolution*: contracts are hard to change; use upgrade strategies, versioned interfaces, and feature flags in off-chain code (see =15_deployment-and-upgrades.org=).
- *Observability*: on-chain events, indexers, metrics, and alerts (see =17_reliability-and-monitoring.org=).
- *Regulatory and operational constraints*: jurisdictional restrictions, sanctions screening, record keeping.

** 5. Selecting chains and infrastructure
- *Chain selection criteria*: security model and decentralization stage (for L2s, see the stages framework in file 11), liquidity and users, tooling and wallet support, fee levels and predictability, finality, EVM compatibility, data availability, ecosystem and counterparty risk, regulatory posture, exit options (bridges, withdrawal delays), and maturity of the client and infrastructure ecosystem.
- *RPC and node strategy*: multiple providers with failover and quorum reads for critical data; self-hosted nodes for control, with the operational cost; rate limits and costs; dedicated endpoints for bots.
- *Build versus buy*: indexing, oracle, custody, monitoring, and relaying can be bought; evaluate vendor risk, lock-in, data portability, and what happens if the vendor fails.
- *Dependency risk for integrated protocols*: treat each integrated protocol (a DEX, a lending market, a bridge, an oracle) as a third-party dependency with its own admin keys, upgrade rights, and failure modes; list them in a dependency register with owners and monitoring.

** 6. Reference architecture sketch: a vault protocol
- *Contracts*: a vault (ERC-4626 style), a strategy adapter layer with allowlisted external protocols, a price-source adapter (oracle with staleness checks), a parameter and role manager, a timelock and multisig for administration, and an emergency pause with narrowly scoped powers.
- *Off-chain*: an indexer producing positions and history; a keeper for harvests and rebalances with idempotent, simulated transactions; a risk monitor watching oracle deviation, utilization, and external protocol health; alerting and runbooks.
- *Frontend and wallet integration*: reads via multicall and an indexer, optimistic UI backed by transaction tracking, simulation before signing, clear signing for approvals.
- *Controls*: deposit caps, a guarded launch, audits and a bounty, upgrade through timelock with public notice, and a decentralization roadmap.
- *Failure modes planned*: oracle failure (fallback and pause), external protocol failure (withdraw to idle), RPC failure (multi-provider), chain halt or reorg (confirmation policy), key compromise (role separation, rotation), and frontend compromise (integrity checks, alternative interfaces).

** 7. Architecture review for Web3 systems
Use the review method of the Software Architecture notes with Web3-specific questions:
- Who can move funds, change rules, or stop the system, and what constrains them?
- What are the upgrade and pause paths, and how are keys held?
- What happens if each external dependency lies, stalls, or disappears (oracle, bridge, sequencer, RPC)?
- What is the worst-case loss in one transaction, and what caps it?
- Which invariants hold across all states, and who tests them?
- How do users exit if the team is gone or hostile?
- What are the chain-level assumptions (finality, reorg depth, fee spikes)?
- How does the design evolve: new tokens, new chains, new oracle sources, contract migrations?
- Which parts are irreversible, and what decision records justify them?

** 8. Self-check
1. Draw the architecture of a dApp you know and mark every party that can censor, freeze, or alter user funds or data.
2. Explain why an upgradeable contract governed by a multisig is not "trustless", and what you would publish to make its trust model clear.
3. A product wants to run its matching engine off-chain with on-chain settlement. What are the trust assumptions and failure modes, and how do users protect themselves?
4. Compare a relayer-based gasless design with an ERC-4337 smart account design.
5. Which dependencies would you place in a dependency register for a lending market integration?
6. What does a walkaway test look like for your system?

** 9. Exercises
- Warm-up: produce a trust table for an existing protocol from its public documentation and contracts; verify admin powers by reading the contracts on a block explorer.
- Core: design the architecture of a simple NFT marketplace (listing, buying, royalties), choosing what is on-chain, what is off-chain, and how orders are signed and settled; list failure modes.
- Core: write an architecture document (context, containers, trust boundaries, failure modes, decisions) for a vault protocol, using the C4 and ADR practices of the Software Architecture notes.
- Core: design the multi-provider RPC strategy for a service, including health checks, failover, and quorum reads for critical calls.
- Stretch: design a progressive decentralization roadmap for a protocol with specific powers removed at specific milestones, with measurable criteria.
- Stretch: threat-model a frontend supply chain (dependencies, hosting, DNS, wallet prompts) and propose controls.

** What to remember
- A dApp is a hybrid system; the architecture is defined by trust boundaries and by what the chain enforces.
- Put the minimum on-chain, and make every admin power, dependency, and single point of failure explicit.
- Decentralization claims must match the actual powers of keys, sequencers, RPCs, and frontends.
- Plan for failure of every external dependency, and for users to exit.
- Apply normal architecture discipline, and weigh irreversibility heavily.
